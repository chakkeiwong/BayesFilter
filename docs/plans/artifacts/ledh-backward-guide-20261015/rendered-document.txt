   Younis Kernel Mixtures and Model-Score Estimation
From the LEDH–OT–GenUT Filter to Corrected Mixtures
                                   BayesFilter research note

                                         14 October 2026


                                              Abstract
     A differentiable particle filter can compute the derivative of its own finite likelihood accurately
while estimating the underlying model score poorly. This distinction explains both the progress
and the negative results of the BayesFilter investigation of Younis and Sudderth’s kernel mixtures.
The original observation sidecar led to a complete kernelized-observation recursion and then
to an all-components, raw-importance-weight resampling reference. The integrated derivatives
pass their declared checks, but the calibrated resampling construction has higher model-score
mean squared error than the canonical filter in both tested matrix-Gaussian scopes. We retain
these results and, for models with regular transition densities, derive a different use of the same
mixture ideas: construct evaluable proposals, retain the original transition and observation
densities in the importance correction, and estimate the observed-data score by averaging
complete-data scores over possible ancestors. This smoothing recursion does not apply as written
to degenerate DSGE transitions. For those models we instead derive a disturbance-coordinate
filter, its analytical state sensitivities, and an unbiased unnormalized likelihood–derivative
pair. Propositions and proofs establish the importance correction, the particle induction, exact
reference control variates, and conditional integration. A moving-support Gaussian example
verifies the resulting score identity. Normalization can still introduce finite-particle bias, and
long-horizon variance remains unresolved. Selective pathwise and importance-weight derivatives
supply additional variance-reduction candidates.
    The unbiasedness statement in the Younis–Sudderth construction has a more specific target.
It is an importance-sampling identity for the gradient of a KDM expectation with the current
proposal held fixed. When the integrand is a conditional log likelihood, the resulting quantity is
an expected conditional log likelihood. It is not, in general, the log of the observed-data marginal
likelihood, whose finite-particle score also contains a random normalization. The note documents
the implemented finite programs, the reasons for each change in direction, the complete proposed
recursions, and the experiments needed to distinguish improved model-score accuracy from
implementation correctness. The new combination is a research proposal; its finite-particle bias
and computational value remain to be measured.

1    The model score and the finite filter
The scientific question is how to estimate the score of a state-space model accurately enough to
support inference about its parameters. The intended BayesFilter LEDH calculation uses LEDH
particle flow, PF–PF importance weights, entropy-regularized OT, Contract–E moment restoration,
and GenUT higher-moment correction with two trust-region caps. These mechanisms define a
particular finite calculation. They are useful starting points for a proposal, but they do not define
what the exact model score must be.
    This note grew out of the historical “Section 3.6” observation-sidecar document. That earlier
note remains in the isolated section-3-6-prior-weight-sidecar worktree, under docs/papers/
section_3_6_prior_weight_sidecar/. The present manuscript, docs/papers/ledh_younis_kd
m_score/ledh_younis_kdm_score.tex, is its broader successor. It brings together the subsequent
implementation, results, and the September 11 change in research question. The implementation
history is tied to research commit 804616e3; differences in the September comparison checkout,
5cc59cfa, are identified in Section 13. These are historical snapshots, not the current integration
checkout.
    As of 14 October 2026, the separate controlled-SMC and Younis kernel-mixture replications
have completed their declared, bounded released-source checks. This gives the project two tools
to test: learned lookahead proposals from controlled SMC, and evaluable mixture proposals and
mixture-expectation derivatives from KDM. It does not yet establish that combining either tool
with LEDH improves the model score. The controlled-SMC replication is also distinct from the
original iterated auxiliary particle filter, whose replication remains deferred. Section 7.2 explains the
difference. While the LEDH tuning procedure is unavailable, the owner’s temporary development
baseline fixes transport regularization at ϵ = 1 and the number of Sinkhorn iterations at 5. This is
a fixed, untuned comparison setting, not a validated optimum for every model.

    There are consequently several possible repairs. Missing derivatives of the state, covariance,
initial law, or sampling distribution require an implementation or estimator correction. A reset or
kernel that changes the filtering measure requires a correction to that approximation or an explicit
new target. A valid but noisy integral requires better integration. The development below separates
these explanations before selecting the next method. A zero-mean control variate addresses the last
problem; it does not remove an arbitrary bias already present in its baseline.

at every resampled location creates all-pairs interactions. The papers’ linear-cost inference path
sets forward resampling weights to uniform and does not compute these gradients. It cannot be
transplanted to a score path without a new approximation and error analysis.

5.4   Why the next direction changes
The tested all-IWSG candidate is rejected for the two matrix-Gaussian scopes. Its derivative agrees
with its declared finite program, yet its model-score MSE is higher. In the smaller scope, the
descriptive error mean and variance changed from −1.08143 and 2.77816 for the canonical score to
−1.32100 and 2.71290 for Phase 4B. In the larger scope they changed from −0.98767 and 2.89289
to −1.57710 and 7.66270. The second scope therefore shows both more squared error in the mean
and more variation across paths. These across-path decompositions are descriptive; they do not
separately estimate the conditional bias at each fixed dataset.
    The result is not adequately explained by saying that the KDM derivative is different from the
atom derivative. Both are candidates for approximating the exact model score, and a different
estimator could have smaller error. The more useful question is why the new approximation should
be closer to the model. Adding Gaussian observation covariance changes the observation factor.
Adding a second reset changes future particles. Evaluating narrow kernels with fixed-location


                                                  17
importance gradients can amplify noise. None of these operations, by itself, corrects the original
model-score bias.
    The earlier restriction to the executed finite derivative made unrelated score recursions appear
inadmissible. That restriction protected the meaning of the canonical API, but it was too narrow as
a research objective. A Fisher-score estimator can be a valid candidate for the underlying model
without being the derivative of the existing OT-reset scalar. It must be named and validated
accordingly. Likewise, a centered control variate can reduce variance without removing the bias
of its baseline. The distinction changes what an experiment must measure; it does not relax the
mathematics.
    One historical cost argument also needs correction. The Section 3.6 memo grouped PaRIS
with inherently quadratic ancestor calculations. The exact ancestor sum is quadratic, but PaRIS
replaces that sum with sampled backward ancestors and has an expected linear-cost result under
stated mixing and acceptance assumptions [Olsson and Westerborn, 2014, Section 3.1, Algorithm 2;
Section 3.2.4, Theorem 10]. Those assumptions do not hold automatically in every repository model,
particularly when transition densities are singular or backward acceptance becomes small. “Always
quadratic” was therefore an incorrect cost argument. Correcting that argument does not remove
the structural obstruction from degenerate transitions: sampling backward ancestors cannot create
transitions that the model forbids. A comparison with ancestor-averaged methods is a question for
models whose transition and backward-conditioning assumptions hold.
    For those regular-transition models, a reference comparison assigns different jobs to three
components. An evaluable mixture proposes particles. The original model densities specify their
importance correction. A posterior-score recursion averages over the ancestor uncertainty that
remains. Selective pathwise and importance-weight gradients are an additional way to reduce
variance when mixture expectations must be differentiated. This combination has not been tested
here. Its components have explicit roles in the model-score calculation, but that does not make it a
general solution for the degenerate DSGE target. The support obstruction in Section 9.1 must be
resolved before proposing a score recursion for that target.

7.2   Controlled SMC and the replicated lookahead tool
Controlled SMC provides a concrete way to learn the positive functions used in a twisted proposal.
Heng et al. [2020, Sections 4.2–4.3, equations (20)–(21), Algorithms 2–3] approximate a backward
dynamic-programming recursion by least-squares regression. The fitted quantity is the negative
logarithm of a policy correction. Starting at the final observation and working backward, each
regression includes the next fitted correction through an expectation under the current twisted
transition. A subsequent SMC pass uses the refined policy, with the corresponding normalizing
factors and potential corrections. Fitting a useful lookahead function and retaining these exact
corrections are separate requirements: the former controls efficiency; the latter preserves the specified
target.
    This is the controlled-SMC method that has been replicated in the project. The October source
checks establish agreement only within their declared released-source and input/failure scope; they
are not a reproduction of all paper experiments. In particular, the recorded Lorenz covariance failure
remains a failure, not a completed successful Lorenz experiment. Controlled SMC and the original
iAPF both exploit twisting, but their fitted quantities and policy updates are not interchangeable.
The controlled-SMC closure does not close the original-iAPF task discussed next.
    For LEDH, the immediate proposal experiment is to use this learned lookahead to allocate
ancestors or construct an evaluable proposal, while retaining the physical model in the numerator
of (83) or (85). The actual joint or marginal sampling law determines the denominator. This
integration still needs implementation and comparison; copying a controlled-SMC policy onto
LEDH without its normalizers would not supply the stated correction. The fitted policy is frozen
before a score comparison. Derivatives of the resulting parameter-dependent proposal must still be
included wherever the declared finite-program derivative requires them. KDM can then provide an
evaluable mixture or a mixture-expectation derivative within that specified calculation; its local
importance-weight identity alone does not establish an unbiased normalized model score.

7.3   The iterated auxiliary particle filter
The iterated auxiliary particle filter (iAPF) constructs the lookahead rather than assuming that it is

7.5   Alternative covariance geometries
Covariance is a proposal geometry, not a correction to the model. The program will distinguish the
following matrices at each proposal step:

                       PtQ = the covariance supplied by the physical transition,                    (105)
                     PtUKF = the per-particle UKF covariance,                                       (106)
                    PtKDM = the weighted covariance of the continuous mixture,                      (107)
                    PtLEDH = the local flow/GenUT covariance,                                       (108)
                    PtSGQF = the covariance projected from a quadrature cloud.                      (109)

An optional hybrid has the form Pthyb = αPtUKF + (1 − α)PtSGQF , with α tuned on calibration data
and frozen on the claim scope. Every matrix is checked for symmetry, positive definiteness, scale,
and condition number. If it changes the sampling distribution, the resulting qt and its derivatives
must change consistently; a covariance label cannot be added to a weight after the samples have
been drawn.

7.7   Systematic proposal test
The proposal study is a factorial comparison, with the score estimator held fixed while proposal
quality is measured. Its proposal families are bootstrap, locally adapted, KDM, LEDH/GenUT,
twisted PF, iAPF, SGQF-guided, and defensive mixtures. Crossed factors are lookahead (none,
fixed one-step, iterated ψ), covariance (P Q , UKF, KDM, LEDH, SGQF, and calibrated hybrid),
particle count, horizon, and observation regime. Model-corrected normalizer MSE is the primary

                                                        28
criterion for a normalizer-accuracy claim. A better-score claim requires lower oracle score MSE
at the declared particle or compute budget, after the proposal identity and target-preservation
tests pass. Coefficient of variation of zbt , effective sample size, and weight tails explain the result;
they do not replace score error as the criterion. The linear-Gaussian/Kalman model is the first
oracle-bearing check; nonlinear regular-transition models and high-dimensional SGQF cases follow.
    Hard vetoes are a missing or non-integrable proposal density, support failure, non-finite or
incorrectly corrected weights, use of an approximate SGQF or twisted likelihood as the target, and
a claim route that cannot be traced to the declared proposal. A paired uncertainty analysis is
required before calling a candidate better. A passing proposal test establishes a viable proposal
arm; it does not establish an unbiased score.

9.1   Why degenerate transitions obstruct this recursion
Consider a state equation with fewer independent shocks than state coordinates,

                           xt = Aθ xt−1 + Gθ εt ,        εt ∼ N (0, Ir ),                        (126)

    The AR(2) example in (174) makes the failure concrete. A child drawn from ancestor a satisfies

The proposed history average then collapses to the sampled genealogy. It does not recover the
alternative histories needed to repair genealogical degeneracy. PaRIS changes how backward
ancestors are sampled; it cannot give positive probability to these incompatible transitions.
    This obstruction concerns the conditional transitions between fixed particles. The exact predictive
marginal can still be full dimensional after integrating over earlier continuous state uncertainty, as
the same AR(2) example shows. That fact does not make the finite mixture of distinct conditional
supports full dimensional. An ambient Gaussian proposal assigns zero probability to their union,
so it cannot provide the exact importance correction for that finite singular mixture. Adding full-
dimensional process noise changes the model; smoothing the finite predictive mixture into an ambient
KDM changes the approximation. Neither operation establishes the displayed model-corrected
recursion for the original degenerate transition.
    Innovation coordinates are a possible starting point for a different derivation. They require the
joint likelihood to be written in those coordinates, including the initial law and the dependence of
the state map on θ. Substituting an innovation density for fθ (xt | xt−1 ) in the existing backward
weights is not such a derivation. In particular, moving support contributes state-map derivatives to
the observation factors, as (178) demonstrates.
    Some degenerate models admit an exact reduction to common stochastic coordinates, or another
specially derived smoothing method. The obstruction therefore does not rule out every form of
smoothing. It does rule out the ordinary all-ancestor recursion above as a general method for the
degenerate DSGE target. A successful full-rank Gaussian comparison would establish no extension
to that target. The disturbance construction below instead needs its own support and differentiation
assumptions.

9.3   Using both past and future information
Younis and Sudderth’s smoother suggests a complementary proposal design. Their equation (18)
draws from a mixture of forward and backward predictive approximations, allowing either set of
observations to nominate a state. Their equation (19) retains a generative correction, including an
auxiliary reference density; equation (20) replaces its numerator with a learned discriminative weight
[Younis and Sudderth, 2024, Section 4]. The distinction matters here. A learned state predictor is
not automatically a model posterior, and multiplying two posterior approximations can count prior
information twice unless the appropriate reference-density factor is included.
    For transition parameters, the score contains functions of (Xt−1 , Xt ), so single-time smoothing
marginals alone are insufficient. A Younis-inspired smoother must supply adjacent-state or path
information and the original model correction. Its most direct role is therefore an evaluable proposal
for the expectation in (121), followed by model-based weighting, rather than replacement of that
expectation by the paper’s supervised state-prediction loss. A learned proposal would require its
own target-specific training protocol and downstream validation.

11     Other options and their place in the investigation
Analytic marginalization should come before additional approximation when the model permits it.
Section 6 gives the one-step Gaussian example. A conditionally linear-Gaussian state block can
similarly be integrated rather than sampled, provided its conditional model is retained exactly. A
Gaussian approximation to a nonlinear block is a different choice and needs its own error assessment.
Neither a good fit to state observations nor an appealing proposal density establishes that the
downstream score is accurate.
    More particles, paired random designs, and randomized quasi-Monte Carlo are important
equal-compute alternatives. They can improve integration without changing the scientific target,
but they cannot restore a missing derivative or correct a filtering approximation with persistent
bias. The comparison must distinguish a better estimator from simply spending more work on it.
Sparse mixtures, low-rank density approximations, and approximate backward sums likewise require
separate bias and cost measurements; they are not algebraically equivalent to the full mixture merely
because they produce finite values.
    The literature audit identifies several named comparators that should be added before the score
study is treated as complete. Nemeth, Fearnhead, and Mihaylova use kernel density approximation
and Rao–Blackwellisation for a linear-cost score and observed-information estimator [Nemeth et al.,
2016]. This is the closest published precedent for the KDM row in the present program. Its


                                                 44
bandwidth, kernel, and regular-transition assumptions must be carried into the experiment; a
positive-bandwidth estimate is not an exact finite-particle score. For additive complete-data terms,
the sequential smoother of Fearnhead, Wyncoll, and Tawn [Fearnhead et al., 2010] and the PaRIS
recursion of Olsson and Westerborn [Olsson and Westerborn, 2017] provide explicit linear-cost
alternatives to a generic “backward pair” label. PaRIS’s number of backward draws and its mixing
conditions are part of the method, not implementation details.
    A qualitatively different regular-model comparator is the coupled conditional particle smoother
of Jacob, Lindsten, and Schön [Jacob et al., 2020]. Rhee–Glynn debiasing of coupled CPF chains
gives an unbiased smoothing expectation under meeting and moment assumptions; the authors
explicitly note that Fisher’s identity then yields an unbiased score when the transition density is
tractable. The construction has random cost and does not make a singular or intractable DSGE
transition score exact. It should be tested as a separate debiasing arm rather than folded into a
variance- minimising linear combination of two biased estimates.
    The continuous-likelihood econometric literature, including Malik–Pitt and DeJong et al. [Malik
and Pitt, 2011, DeJong et al., 2013], is also a required comparator for the motivation behind
differentiable resampling. A continuous particle likelihood can remove a resampling discontinuity
while still defining a different finite-particle measure, so its derivative requires the same target and
normalization audit used here. When a transition can be simulated but its density is unavailable,
iterated filtering [Ionides et al., 2011] supplies a separate artificial-parameter-noise approximation
with an explicit bias–variance–mixing trade-off. It is the natural simulation-only comparator for the
DSGE branch, but it is not an exact finite-N score.
    Twisted particle filters [Whiteley and Lee, 2014] and multilevel particle filters [Jasra et al., 2017]
belong in proposal and discretization variance studies. Twisting preserves a likelihood through a
change-of-measure correction, while multilevel estimators telescope coupled discretization levels;
neither paper supplies a score identity. A score-level telescope or coupling must therefore be proved
before either can support an unbiased-score claim. Finally, variational particle objectives and
learned smoothers remain a target-boundary issue: an ELBO or a learned conditional objective
cannot be reported as the observed-data score without a separate measure identity.
    Bandwidth or particle-count extrapolation is another possible bias-reduction strategy, but it
requires a justified expansion. A presumed h2 smoothing bias or 1/N score bias cannot be used across
every nonsmooth, constrained, or resampling model without evidence. Randomized telescoping is

14.5    Executable order and scientific scope
The bounded implementation is an optional single-cloud proposal extension, not an alternative
LEDH filter. Its order is:
     1. Freeze a validated time-indexed guide. Its last future-only factor is constant. Fix the existing
        randomness and numerical controls.
     2. Predict physical UKF moments and compute each transition anchor and its total tangent
        from the current reset state.
     3. Evaluate H, b, Ḣ, ḃ; generate U, U̇ from the guided Gaussian. With guidance disabled use the
        original transition draw.
     4. Run the shared LEDH flow and its analytical tangent. Evaluate the unchanged physical
        transition and observation densities and the actual guided Gaussian denominator in (203).
     5. Accumulate the finite log normalizer and derivative; update physical UKF covariances. Execute
        the existing OT, Contract E and GenUT dual-cap reset with all tangents. These particles are
        the next parents.
An OT barycentre has no unique sampled ancestor. Neither its transport matrix nor the coefficients
of a kernel mixture are thereby a model backward-smoothing kernel. No ancestral tracing or
Fisher-score substitution is part of this implementation.
      The first evidence is engineering evidence: disabled-guide parity, Gaussian square-completion
checks, moving-anchor mixture tangents, and finite differences of the complete same-value recursion.
The development comparator uses ϵ = 1 and five Sinkhorn iterations; balancing counts, ridge,
correction and cap settings are reported independently. These are untuned settings. CPU-only small
reference tests do not establish GPU/XLA readiness. Pilot fitting on actual models, equal-compute
repeated-seed score comparisons, per-scope tuning, batch-native consumer coverage and any scientific
or default promotion remain subsequent work. In particular, accepting coefficient arrays is not
evidence that the replicated fitting endpoints have been integrated or that their output represents
ht .


15     The next discriminating comparison
For models with regular transition densities, the reference question is whether model-corrected
mixture proposals and ancestor averaging reduce model-score error at equal compute. This is a
restricted comparison, not an implementation plan for degenerate DSGE transitions. A method
intended for that target must first pass the conditional-support analysis in Section 9.1; more
accurate results on full-rank Gaussian models cannot remove that obstruction. Proposal quality,
score construction, and mixture-gradient variance must be separated. Otherwise a successful run



                                                  58
could be attributed to the wrong mechanism, just as a passing derivative check previously obscured
a changed scalar.
    The smallest useful sequence begins with three calculations. First, compare (71) and (78)
with direct Gaussian integration on a fixed incoming cloud. Second, compare the complete-data
and backward-recursion scores with exact enumeration on a small finite-state model, including a
parameter-dependent initial law. Third, compare IWSG, centered IWSG, and (166) for the same
declared mixture expectation. These calculations test identities and failure mechanisms; their
particle counts need only be large enough to exercise the operation being tested. They are not
score-quality campaigns or reasons to select a production particle count.
    The subsequent accuracy study should use a linear-Gaussian model with nondegenerate transi-
tions and an independent Kalman score, a short Gaussian-mixture model with evaluable transition
densities whose modes can be enumerated exactly, and a scalar nonlinear model with a regular tran-
sition density and a numerically converged quadrature score. Kalman checks the basic integration
and complete derivatives. The mixture model tests the separated modes that motivate Younis’s
method. The nonlinear model tests how proposal approximation affects the result. A one-step or
unimodal success cannot establish the latter two properties. Models with unresolved initialization
or support identity must be repaired before their score results are interpreted.
    For each model, change one mechanism at a time: ordinary ancestor sampling versus marginal
mixture weights; a genealogical complete-data score versus the full ancestor average; and, where
an expectation derivative is actually used, all-IWSG versus the selective estimator. Include the
enhanced combination only after the component comparisons. The exact full-mixture reference
remains necessary when evaluating a later sparse or sampled approximation.
    The cheap alternatives are constructed from the model before claim data are examined. A
bootstrap PF proposes from the physical transition and uses a proper complete-data score estimator.
A locally adapted PF uses the current observation to guide each ancestor’s proposal and keeps the
exact importance weight. In nearly linear regimes, EKF and UKF approximate-likelihood scores
test whether inexpensive Gaussian propagation already suffices; their derivatives must include the
full declared Gaussian approximation. A fixed-setting LEDH filter with ϵ = 1 and five Sinkhorn
iterations supplies the current project development comparator. All other numerical controls must
be declared and held fixed within a comparison. This temporary owner-selected setting does not
replace the scope-specific tuning required before a confirmatory or default-readiness claim. The
exact Kalman, mixture-enumeration, or quadrature solution supplies the oracle where it is available;
an approximate reference must carry its own uncertainty. Neither a weak fixed-ancestry derivative
nor this untuned LEDH baseline should stand in for all conventional particle-score methods.
    Evaluate these comparisons conditionally. Weak and strong observations probe proposal concen-
tration; overlapping and separated modes probe mixture gradients; low and high persistence probe
loss of ancestor diversity; short and long horizons probe accumulation; near-zero and large score
components probe the error scale. These situations must be defined from the model and research
question before using validation data. Failure against a cheap alternative in a salient situation
rejects promotion under the repository’s sanity-check rule, even if the method wins an unconditional
average. The adversaries are falsification checks, not extra tuning targets.
    For dataset d and independent filter randomization r, denote the estimate by sbd,r and the
oracle by sd . Replication within each fixed dataset estimates bd and Vd from (3); the distribution
over datasets measures generalization. Pair methods within datasets and respect this nesting in
confidence intervals. A path bootstrap with only one filter realization per path cannot estimate
each dataset’s conditional bias. Freeze parameter coordinate scales before comparison, because a
relative error divided by a nearly zero oracle score is unstable. Change N and T independently, and
report both equal-particle and equal-measured-compute comparisons.

proposed new calculations is promoted by this document.

and complete initialization, an independently checked score recursion, and a powered comparison
against simple alternatives. The strongest alternative explanation for any improvement is better
proposal adaptation or more effective computation, rather than the gradient identity. The component
comparisons distinguish those explanations. Loss to a tuned conventional score estimator at equal
compute would overturn the practical priority of the new combination without invalidating its
algebra.
